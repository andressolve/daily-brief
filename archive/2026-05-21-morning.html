The Uranium Directive

Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei issued a directive that Iran's ~400+ kg of near-weapons-grade (~60%) enriched uranium must not be sent abroad. This is not a Foreign Ministry position or a negotiating gambit from a mid-level official — it is the Supreme Leader's decree, which in Iran's constitutional architecture carries the weight of law.

Why this matters structurally: The US has been explicit that HEU transfer abroad is the non-negotiable nuclear demand. Trump said on April 12: "most points were agreed to, but the only point that mattered, NUCLEAR, was not." Khamenei's directive doesn't just reject this demand — it elevates the rejection to the highest authority in the Iranian system. For Mojtaba to reverse this would cost him authority he hasn't yet fully consolidated as the new Supreme Leader. This is a commitment device, not a bargaining chip.

Bear case on the bear case: It's possible this is Mojtaba establishing a public hardline position that gives him room to make private concessions later (the classic "I fought but was forced to compromise" playbook). Iran's negotiating history includes maximalist public stances that soften in back channels. But even if this is posture, it raises the cost of any deal that includes uranium transfer — Mojtaba would need a face-saving reframing (e.g., "monitored enrichment" vs. "transfer").

The Military Rebuild

CNN reports, citing US intelligence: "The Iranians have exceeded all timelines the IC had for reconstitution." Drone production already restarted during the ceasefire. Surviving missile launchers revised UP to two-thirds (from lower prior estimates). Full drone capability reconstitution estimated at ~6 months.

The dual-pressure dynamic: This creates opposing forces. For diplomacy: Iran rebuilding = more urgency to get a deal before Iran is back to full strength. For Israel: the target is hardening daily, shrinking the window for effective military action. Netanyahu can tell his cabinet: "every day we wait, the target gets harder." These two forces — diplomatic urgency vs. military urgency — point in different directions and make the situation more volatile, not less.
